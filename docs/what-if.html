What if New York looked?

What if policymakers, legislators, health agencies, hospitals, law enforcement and research institutions focused on femicide in the sex trade, as they should? The question underneath is why they do not. If they did, the violence and death inside the trade, and the profits above it, would be visible.

What if law enforcement counted?

Attempted murders and murders of people in the sex trade, recorded as such, categorised by the victim's sex and gender and by the demographics of the perpetrator. Today the domestic incident report has no box for it.

What if every precinct reported the trade on its streets?

Police precincts across the state quantifying and reporting the commercial sex establishments in their area: strip clubs, illicit "massage" parlours, hotel and motel prostitution sites, brothels operating as clubs.

What if hospitals asked?

Emergency rooms and hospitals reporting and collecting data on violence against patients in the sex trade, the way they report gunshot wounds.

What if the state followed the money?

The Department of Taxation and Finance, the Comptroller or another agency collecting financial information from commercial sex establishments, including legal strip clubs. New York City does not isolate revenue for these businesses, although reports put high-profile Manhattan strip clubs at $5 million to $15 million a year each.

What if researchers had the data?

Universities and public health institutes with access to the counts above, able to say how many, who, where and what it costs. This platform is a prototype of what that would show.

What if the law named it?

A femicide definition that includes the sex trade, with a reporting duty. Every "what if" above follows from it.

Preventing and ending sexual violence and femicide

Two ways to picture the same idea. In a game of chutes and ladders, the ladders are what carries a woman up and out of harm, and the chutes are what drops her back. In the Swiss cheese model, each law, policy and community intervention is a slice with holes in it; stack enough slices and the holes stop lining up, and a perpetrator can no longer pass straight through.

Ladders

Enforcing the patronizing laws that exist. Strengthening them: the Sex Trade Survivors Justice and Equality Act. Targeting the online sexual exploitation sites where the buyers post. Valuing women and girls as equal human beings. A culture, from Hollywood to the news desk, that stops normalising prostitution.

Chutes

Systemic loopholes. Harmful cultural norms. Law enforcement and institutional failures. Societal complicity. Dehumanisation of, or indifference to, women in the sex trade.

The playable version, with the Gilgo Beach murders as the case study, is on the backlog: at each square, what law or system could have caught the perpetrator sooner. Models and examples CATW points to: the Swiss cheese model applied to harassment in Mind the Gaps (Southern California Law Review, 2025); RAND's gun policy comparison tool; The Violence Project's mass shooter database.
