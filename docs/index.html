Femicide in New York State

Around the world, femicide is an epidemic, including in the sex trade. We call on New York to be the first US state to codify femicide into law.

What is femicide?

The United Nations defines femicide as an intentional killing with a gender-related motivation, and the most extreme and brutal manifestation of violence against women and girls.

This project is inspired by the New York City Coalition to End Femicide, initiated by Sanctuary for Families, New York's leading service provider and advocate for survivors of domestic violence, sex trafficking and related forms of gender violence. The initiative includes different forms of femicide:*

Intimate partner femicide

Current or former partners.

Non-partner femicide

Family members, acquaintances, or strangers.

Reproductive-related femicide

Pregnancy, postpartum status, or reproductive control.

Sex trade-related femicide

Prostitution and commercial sexual exploitation.

Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women

Colonial violence and systemic neglect.

Transfemicide

Misogyny, transphobia, and racism.

*These classifications come from Sanctuary for Families.

Why are deaths related to sex trafficking and prostitution forms of femicide?

Out of an estimated 40 to 42 million people in prostitution worldwide, roughly 80%, about 4 out of 5, are women and girls, disproportionately of color. Prostitution is an element of the sex trade, an umbrella term that includes escort services, illegal massage parlors, pornography, strip clubs, sugar dating, and street prostitution.

Currently, no government or other agency in the U.S. or in New York systematically counts the number of women and girls killed in the sex trade by sex buyers, pimps, and traffickers.

Sources: 40 to 42 million (attributed to the UN, 2009): Fondation Scelles, Current assessment of the state of prostitution (2013), page 5. The 80% share of women is not in that document; it is reported by The World (2017) citing the foundation's 2012 release. The first step for New York is to name and codify femicide

Homicide is a crime under the law. However, given the reported high statistics on gender-based murders, it is important for every jurisdiction to collect data, identify, and prosecute crimes men commit against women and girls that are specifically gender-based.

Collecting data will help illuminate femicide as a systemic failure, rather than unrelated, private tragedies, and an issue that urgently deserves legislative action.

Twenty-three countries name femicide as a crime, and seven more define it or punish it more severely. The United States and New York State do not. Why the FBI and the others do not count it.

Where femicide is codified in law

30countries address femicide in law, Costa Rica in 2007 to Italy in 2025: 23 name it as a crime, Belgium defines it without a separate crime, and 6 punish gender-motivated homicide more severely without naming it. Naming the crime is what makes it countable. The World Bank counts 29 economies but does not publish its list. Click any country for its note; click a legend entry for its list.

Spain is shaded with the penalty countries because the World Bank names it as the first, in 1996, to make the victim's gender an aggravating circumstance; it also runs a national count, which its note explains. Sweden only counts, with no provision in its law, so it stays dotted. A country left grey had no femicide provision in those three sources; it may still, like France, punish a gender-motivated homicide more severely, and the World Bank's own count of 29 is not published country by country.

In the United States, femicide cases are treated as isolated incidents, not as part of a larger pattern and evidence of a culture's inherent misogyny and normalization of violence against women and girls.
