What gets counted, and who doesn't

Femicide across the world, and in the sex trade, and the case for New York to become the first US state to name it in law.


### What is femicide?

The intentional killing of a woman or girl because she is female. Roughly three dozen countries define it in their criminal codes; the United States and New York State do not.

### The gap this project exists to close

Roughly three dozen countries define femicide in their criminal codes. The United States and New York State do not. No agency counts the women killed in the sex trade. When those deaths are reported at all, the language used often hides what happened.


**Countries that name femicide in law**

Most are in Latin America and Europe. Naming the crime is what makes it countable, and counting is what makes it a policy problem rather than a series of private tragedies.


**Women killed in the sex trade in New York: unknown, because uncounted**

The number is not zero and it is not small. It is unknown, because no agency records it. The only systematic record in the country is a volunteer database, Women Count USA, built from news reports, social media and obituaries. This project exists to replace the question mark with a count.

The count of countries recognising femicide is an approximate figure from CATW's project brief. Women Count USA is maintained by Dawn Wilcox at womencountusa.org.

### Time served: sex crimes against white-collar crimes

The comparison people expect is that rapists get off lightly against tax evaders. The federal numbers say the opposite, and that is the point: when a sexual abuser is sentenced, the sentence is long. He is almost never sentenced. Impunity is not in the courtroom; it is in the thousand cases that never reach one.


**Average federal sentence, fiscal year 2024**

share sentenced to prison: federal sexual abuse offenders against federal tax offenders. Of fraud, theft and embezzlement offenders the median sentence is 12 months.

# BY THE NUMBERS

By the numbers: who was killed, by whom, where and when

Women Count USA is the only systematic record in the country of women and girls killed by men and boys. Dawn Wilcox has built it by hand since 2016 from news reports, court records, social media and obituaries. It runs from the 1950s to this year, in fifteen working databases. They are shown here exactly as she publishes them, not copied.


### The databases, year by year

Pick a year. The table opens inside this page with every column Dawn Wilcox records: her age, her race, the relationship, his age, cause of death, location type, case status and the sources for each case. Filter and sort inside the table.

Source: Women Count USA: Femicide Accountability Project, Dawn Wilcox, womencountusa.org/the-databases. Copyrighted material, embedded with the share links she publishes. Entries are at different stages of completion.


### Your tally

Filter any table above, read the record count at the bottom of the grid, and log it here with the filter you used. The counts stay in your browser, chart by year, export as a file, and carry into the lost-earnings ledger. The database is never copied; the counting is yours.

Counts are stored only in this browser. Record what you counted and the date on the methodology page's terms: a count is a floor, entries are at various stages of completion, and the live tables change daily.


### The questions we are asking of the data

Each card names a question, the columns that answer it, and how the answer will be shown once aggregation is agreed with Women Count USA and CATW. Until then the embedded tables above are the analysis surface.


**How old were they?**

Age at death, in bands, for all victims and for New York. Girls under 18 separately.


**Who was the perpetrator?**

Relationship to the victim grouped into intimate partner, family, acquaintance, stranger, commercial sexual exploiter and unknown. His age and race where recorded.


**Where were they?**

State and city for every case; the database also keeps an address field for mapping.


**Were they killed in the sex trade?**

The database codes commercial sexual exploitation as a relationship type and as an additional detail. These are the cases New York does not count anywhere.


**What patterns repeat?**

Prior protective orders, strangulation history, threats to kill, break-ups in the weeks before, murder-suicide, firearms.


**What happened to the case?**

Conviction, pending, suspect deceased, unsolved, declined indictment, mishandled.

# WHAT IF…?

What if New York looked?

What if policymakers, legislators, health agencies, hospitals, law enforcement and research institutions focused on femicide in the sex trade, as they should? The question underneath is why they do not. If they did, the violence and death inside the trade, and the profits above it, would be visible.


**What if law enforcement counted?**

Attempted murders and murders of people in the sex trade, recorded as such, categorised by the victim's sex and gender and by the demographics of the perpetrator. Today the domestic incident report has no box for it.


**What if every precinct reported the trade on its streets?**

Police precincts across the state quantifying and reporting the commercial sex establishments in their area: strip clubs, illicit "massage" parlours, hotel and motel prostitution sites, brothels operating as clubs.


**What if hospitals asked?**

Emergency rooms and hospitals reporting and collecting data on violence against patients in the sex trade, the way they report gunshot wounds.


**What if the state followed the money?**

The Department of Taxation and Finance, the Comptroller or another agency collecting financial information from commercial sex establishments, including legal strip clubs. New York City does not isolate revenue for these businesses, although reports put high-profile Manhattan strip clubs at $5 million to $15 million a year each.


**What if researchers had the data?**

Universities and public health institutes with access to the counts above, able to say how many, who, where and what it costs. This platform is a prototype of what that would show.


**What if the law named it?**

A femicide definition that includes the sex trade, with a reporting duty. Every "what if" above follows from it.

### What CATW does

The Coalition Against Trafficking in Women has worked since 1988 to end the trafficking and sexual exploitation of women and girls. Its position is that prostitution and sex trafficking are gender-based violence, and that the law should target the demand, not the women. This site is the data arm of that argument for New York.


**Advocating for strong laws**

Laws that provide access to justice, target demand and ensure women and girls are free from the violence of the sex trade. CATW calls this the Equality Model. Read more


**Raising public awareness**

Presenting trafficking and sexual exploitation as sex-based and gender-based violence, and mobilising the public to press policymakers. Read more


**Supporting survivor leadership**

Partnering with survivors on prevention and protection policy, so the people with the most knowledge lead. Read more


**What CATW is asking New York to do**

Name femicide in state law, and include women killed in the sex tradeThis project

About three dozen countries define femicide in their criminal codes. No US state does. CATW's brief proposes that New York codify sex trafficking and prostitution as forms of femicide, so the deaths are counted and the pattern becomes a policy problem.

Close the gap survivors call the Epstein loopholeCivil justice

Epstein survivors have told New York legislators that his estate argues the statute of limitations makes their claims worth nothing, and that current law bars punitive damages against a deceased abuser's estate. CATW works with survivors pressing to close this.

Pass the Sex Trade Survivors Justice and Equality ActIntroduced 2021

The Equality Model bill for New York, developed with survivors and introduced in 2021: decriminalise the people sold, hold buyers and exploiters accountable, fund exit services. Maine passed the first state version in June 2023. New Yorkers for the Equality Model, with CATW, has carried it since 2019.

Finish what the 2018 law startedS5988A, S8723

In 2018 New York created the crime of sex trafficking of a child, removing the need to prove force, fraud or coercion for anyone under 18. Senator Myrie's S8723 would extend that to adults who are mentally disabled or incapacitated. The force, fraud or coercion test still applies to every other adult.

Make buying sex from a minor a felony everywherePassed

New York's Victims Protection and Child Sex Buyer Accountability Act made buying sex from 15 to 17 year olds a felony rather than a misdemeanour, closing a gap with federal law and other states.

Count the cost, the way France didEconomics

CATW points to a French report on the cost of prostitution to society that persuaded lawmakers there. The economics page on this site starts that accounting for New York.
